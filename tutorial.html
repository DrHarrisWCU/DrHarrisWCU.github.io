Module 1 of 11 · about 25 minutes

Talk to a servo

A hobby servo reads one pulse width. A DYNAMIXEL is a small computer on a shared serial bus, so you send it packets. Each servo has an ID and answers only when addressed.

By the end: build a real Protocol 2.0 packet by hand, decode one, and explain why a servo ignores a damaged packet.
In ATmega terms

This is UART framing you could write yourself: a start pattern, a length, a payload, and a check value. The CRC routine is eight lines of C.

uint16_t crc16(const uint8_t *p, uint16_t n) {
  uint16_t crc = 0;
  while (n--) {
    crc ^= (uint16_t)(*p++) << 8;
    for (uint8_t i = 0; i < 8; i++)
      crc = (crc & 0x8000) ? (crc << 1) ^ 0x8005 : (crc << 1);
  }
  return crc;
}

from math import sin, cos
def tip(yaw, pitch):
    x = 0.01175 + 0.035*sin(pitch)*cos(yaw) - 0.020*sin(yaw)

Module 4 of 11 · about 30 minutes
Close the loop

  
Every servo is a feedback loop. It measures where the horn is, compares it with the goal, and pushes with a torque that grows with the error. You will tune that loop on a simulated XL430 joint.

  
By the end: tune Kp and Kd to a fast, clean step, and explain what delay does to a loop.


  
In ATmega terms
    
This is the PID loop you already wrote, with the I term left out. Kp pushes toward the goal. Kd pushes against speed, so it acts like friction you can dial in. The XL430 stores these as Position P Gain and Position D Gain in its control table.


err = goal - pos;
tau = Kp * err - Kd * vel;      // D acts on measured speed
if (tau >  TAU_MAX) tau =  TAU_MAX;
if (tau < -TAU_MAX) tau = -TAU_MAX;

Take it to your real servo
    
Record the commanded and measured position into a CSV with columns time, cmd, pos. Then run the same fit on real data.


import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

t, cmd, pos = np.loadtxt("step_log.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, unpack=True)

def model(p, t, cmd):
    delay, wn, zeta, vmax = p
    dt = t[1] - t[0]
    th = np.zeros_like(t); w = 0.0
    for k in range(1, len(t)):
        c = np.interp(t[k] - delay, t, cmd)
        w += (wn**2 * (c - th[k-1]) - 2*zeta*wn*w) * dt
        w = np.clip(w, -vmax, vmax)
        th[k] = th[k-1] + w*dt
    return th

fit = least_squares(lambda p: model(p, t, cmd) - pos,
                    x0=[0.01, 30, 0.7, 6.0],
                    bounds=([0, 5, 0.1, 1], [0.05, 100, 2.0, 8]))
print(dict(zip(["delay", "wn", "zeta", "vmax"], fit.x)))

    
Always plot the fit over the data before you trust the numbers.


  
For the mentor: sign-off question

Module 7 of 11 · about 45 minutes
Teach a toy robot to walk

  
Reinforcement learning is trial and error with a score. A policy is a recipe that turns the situation into motor commands. Training nudges the recipe so the score goes up. Here the whole idea fits in a page.

  
By the end: train a gait, see a simulator mislead you, and use a motor model and a smoothness penalty to fix it.


  
What is real and what is a toy
    
The walker has one leg with a swing joint and a lift joint. The body moves forward when the foot is down and the leg sweeps back. The policy is four numbers: swing size, lift size, stride frequency, and the timing between the two joints. Training is random search, not the PPO that Isaac Lab uses, and Isaac Lab trains thousands of robots at once. The ideas carry over. The numbers do not. The motor model uses lag, delay, and a speed limit in the range you measured in Module 5.

What to look for
    
1. Train in the ideal simulator with no penalty. The sim reports a huge distance. Play it on the real robot model and read the real number. 2. Train again with the motor model. The sim's claim now matches reality, so you can trust it. 3. Raise the penalty. Servo effort is how hard the commands whip the motor, which means heat and gear wear. 4. The Kalman filter in one paragraph
      
It is a weighted average that picks the weight from how noisy each source is. If the gyro is steadier than the wheels, trust the gyro more. The "Trust in gyro" slider is that weight, set by hand. In ROS 2, the robot_localization package computes it for you.


K = P / (P + R);        // P: how unsure we are, R: sensor noise
x = x + K * (z - x);    // blend the new measurement in
P = (1 - K) * P;

    
What SLAM adds
      
Neither sensor sees the outside world, so their errors only grow. SLAM compares each lidar scan with the map built so far and pulls the position back into line. When the robot returns to a place it has seen, it spreads the leftover error back over the whole lap. In ROS 2 this is the slam_toolbox package.

Module 10 of 11 · about 45 minutes
The same arm in a second simulator

  
MuJoCo is a free physics engine from Google DeepMind. It installs with one pip command, runs on an ordinary laptop CPU, and appears in many robot-learning papers and tutorials. You will load your arm into it, fix two bugs in the model file, tune its motor, and break its timestep on purpose.

  
By the end: read a MuJoCo model file, run it from Python, and explain why a simulator can blow up.


  
Why use a second simulator
    
Reach. Isaac Lab needs an NVIDIA RTX GPU and a large install. MuJoCo runs anywhere Python runs, so you can try an idea on a laptop in a few minutes.

    
Cross-check. If your fitted motor gives the same step response in two simulators, a mistake in your files is unlikely. If they disagree, one file is wrong. People call this sim-to-sim checking, and it costs an afternoon instead of a broken gearbox.

    
Shared models. MuJoCo Menagerie is a public collection of ready-made robot models, and MuJoCo MJX runs the same engine on GPUs for training. Check each project's repository for what is current before you build on it.

Stuck? Hints, one at a time
      
1. Read the last line of the error. It names a joint and a line. Compare the name with the joint lines near the top of the file.

      
2. Loaded, but the arm stalls near 0.07 rad with no error? Look at the unit of every number on the joint line. A model file has a setting for how it reads angles, and its default is not the one the URDF used.

      
3. Overshooting and ringing? The motor line has a velocity gain, kv. It is the D term from Module 4, written as a motor property. Try values between 0.02 and 0.1.

      
4. To break it, raise timestep above 0.02. To see MuJoCo's own warning instead of a thrashing arm, also delete the forcerange text from both motor lines.

Three habits this lab was built to teach
    
Units first. When a number in a model file looks right and the motion looks wrong, check units before you check physics.

    
Armature. A servo's gearbox makes its rotor feel like a much heavier part than the link it moves. MuJoCo's armature field carries that reflected inertia. Set it to 0 and the simulated joint becomes unrealistically light and touchy.

    
A clamp can hide a fire. The force limit kept the arm from producing NaN at a huge timestep, but the arm was still thrashing. Look at the motion, not only at the absence of errors.

Module 11 of 11 · about 50 minutes
Walk before you learn: quadruped gaits

  
A gait is a schedule that says which feet are on the ground and when. Before you hand a robot to reinforcement learning, you build one gait by hand. It becomes the baseline the learned policy has to beat, and it teaches you what the policy is really searching for.

  
By the end: design a gait that cannot tip over, explain why a trot needs motion to stay up, and turn one moment of the gait into the packet that moves all eight servos.


  
Six words, then the lab
    

      
StanceA foot is on the ground and pushes the body along.

      
SwingA foot is in the air, moving to where the next stance starts.

      
Duty factorThe fraction of one cycle a foot spends in stance. 0.75 means three quarters on the ground.

      
Phase offsetHow far into the cycle a leg is, compared with another leg. The offsets are the gait.

      
Support polygonThe shape you get by connecting the feet that are down, seen from above.

      
Static or dynamicStatic: the center of mass stays inside the polygon, so the robot could pause at any instant and not fall. Dynamic: it stays up only because it keeps moving.

What this lab simplifies
    
Leg geometry. The lab commands each foot's forward and up motion directly and converts it to joint angles with a small-angle shortcut. On your leg the yaw joint swings the whole leg and the pitch joint tilts it, so lifting a foot also moves it sideways. Turning a wanted foot position into joint angles is the forward kinematics of Module 2 run backwards, called inverse kinematics. Replacing the shortcut with it is your first real piece of gait code.

    
Weight shift. The lab slides the center of mass directly. On the robot you get the same effect by changing the other legs' stance positions, or by tilting the body. The idea is the same: be balanced before you lift.

    
No dynamics. There is no friction, slipping, or body inertia. A gait that passes here can still fall on a real floor. That gap is the reason Modules 5 and 7 exist.


  
Where reinforcement learning comes in
    
A learned walking policy has the same job you just did by hand. Each step it reads the body's tilt and turn rates from the IMU, the joint angles, and the commanded speed. It outputs eight joint targets. It gets rewarded for tracking the commanded speed and penalized for effort and jerky commands, which is the smoothness penalty from Module 7. Statically stable crawls are easy to design but slow. Trots and bounds are faster and need balance feedback. That is the kind of behavior a learned policy can discover and a hand-built table cannot.

    
Your hand-built crawl has a speed, a stability margin, and a servo load. Those three numbers are the baseline to beat in a paper.
